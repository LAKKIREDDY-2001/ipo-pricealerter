Mainboard vs SME IPO in India: Key Differences, Risks & Returns

By IPO Pricealerter Research Team Published: 06 November 2024 Updated: 15 November 2024 Reading Time: 8 mins

The Indian capital markets operate on a dual-tier structure for public equity offerings: the traditional Mainboard platform (listing directly on the NSE and BSE primary exchanges) and dedicated SME platforms (BSE SME and NSE Emerge).

In recent years, the SME segment has witnessed unprecedented retail investor attention, accompanied by staggering multi-hundred percent subscription figures and rapid multibagger price surges. However, SME offerings differ radically from mainboard listings in terms of capital requirements, liquidity constraints, minimum ticket sizes, and risk profiles.

What is a Mainboard IPO?

A Mainboard IPO is an offer by an established corporation with a post-issue paid-up equity capital exceeding ₹10 Crores (minimum ₹25 Crores for NSE mainboard listings). These issuers undergo direct vetting by the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI), requiring audited historical profitability records, substantial net worth thresholds, and institutional due diligence.

Once listed on the mainboard, the shares trade freely in units of a single share on standard stock exchanges, ensuring high secondary liquidity.

What is an SME IPO?

Initiated in 2012 following SEBI directives, SME platforms provide small and medium enterprises with growth capital without the prohibitive regulatory costs associated with mainboard filings.

Key attributes of SME issues include:

  • Post-Issue Paid-up Capital: Must not exceed ₹25 Crores.
  • Stock Exchange Vetting: DRHPs are scrutinized directly by BSE SME or NSE Emerge rather than passing through SEBI's direct comment process.
  • 100% Underwritten: The Merchant Banker must underwrite 100% of the issue, holding at least 15% on their own balance sheet.
  • Market Making: Compulsory 3-year market making by designated market makers to provide two-way buy/sell quotes.

Why Are SME Lot Sizes Kept at ₹1 Lakh+?

SEBI deliberately mandated high entry thresholds for SME public issues:

💡 Investor Protection Rationale

Because small enterprises exhibit higher failure rates, shorter operational track records, and lower disclosure scrutiny, the regulator instituted the ₹1 Lakh+ minimum application rule to dissuade inexperienced micro-retail investors from speculating with rent or emergency funds.

Secondary Market Liquidity & Exit Risks in SME Shares

The most significant hidden trap for retail investors in SME stocks is secondary market illiquidity. In a mainboard stock like Tata Motors or Infosys, if you want to sell 1 share, thousands of buyers are available instantly on the order book.

In an SME stock, you cannot sell partial quantities. If your lot size is 2,000 shares, you must find a buyer willing to purchase a block of 2,000 shares at once. In volatile bear markets or during company controversies, market makers frequently widen bid-ask spreads, and lower circuits can trap investors without any exit volume for weeks.

SEBI's Tightening Surveillance on SME Platforms

In response to unusual price surges and corporate governance violations, SEBI and exchanges introduced stringent monitoring tools:

  • Additional Surveillance Measure (ASM) & Graded Surveillance Measure (GSM): Restricting intraday leverage and enforcing 100% upfront margins.
  • Price Band Capping: Imposing daily price bands of 5% or 10% on SME counters to dampen extreme speculative swings.
