Understanding Grey Market Premium (GMP) in IPOs — Realities & Risks

By IPO PriceAlerter Research • 10 min read • Updated: November 2024

If you have ever monitored the financial news or visited retail investment communities during an active public issue, you have encountered the term Grey Market Premium (GMP). Traders frequently discuss numbers like "+₹50 GMP (15%)" or "+₹980 GMP (65%)", using these numbers as a crystal ball to project listing day returns.

However, despite its immense popularity among retail market participants, Grey Market Premium is also one of the most misunderstood and speculative metrics in the Indian financial ecosystem. In this guide, we break down what GMP actually represents, how it is quoted, and why relying exclusively on it can prove hazardous to your portfolio.

What is Grey Market Premium (GMP)?

The Grey Market Premium (GMP) is the unofficial, off-market price at which an IPO's equity shares are bid or offered by private dealers and traders prior to their official listing on recognized stock exchanges like the National Stock Exchange (NSE) or Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE).

It represents the premium (or discount) that buyers are willing to pay over and above the issue price before trading commences officially on the exchange order books.

Estimated Listing Price = Issue Price (Cap) + Grey Market Premium (GMP)

For example, if a company's IPO price band has an upper cap of ₹390, and its current Grey Market Premium is reported at +₹30, the implied or estimated opening listing price in the off-market is ₹420 per share, representing an anticipated +7.69% listing day gain.

How Does the Grey Market Operate in India?

The grey market is an unofficial, informal over-the-counter (OTC) network. It does not operate on exchange matching engines or SEBI-regulated clearing corporations. Instead, transactions are arranged via phone, messaging apps, and private dealer agreements based strictly on mutual counterparty trust.

There are two primary types of informal contracts executed in this unofficial ecosystem:

1. Kostak Rate

The Kostak Rate is the fixed rupee amount an investor receives by selling their entire IPO application to a grey market buyer before allotment is declared, regardless of whether shares are ultimately allotted or not. This allows risk-averse retail bidders to lock in an immediate small cash profit while transferring the allotment lottery risk to the buyer.

2. Subject to Sauda (Sauda Rate)

Under a Subject to Sauda agreement, the deal is valid only if the seller is successfully allotted shares. If no shares are allotted in the computerized lottery, the contract is automatically null and void with zero payment exchanging hands.

Is Grey Market Trading Legal in India?

The Grey Market is completely unofficial and unregulated by SEBI. While it is not strictly prohibited by criminal statute, contracts made within the grey market are legally unenforceable in Indian courts of law. If a counterparty defaults or refuses to transfer funds following extreme market volatility, SEBI, NSE, and BSE provide zero investor protection or compensation mechanisms.

How Reliable is GMP as a Predictor of Listing Day Gains?

Historical market data demonstrates that Grey Market Premium serves as a rough indicator of broad retail sentiment and institutional hype, but it is far from an infallible forecasting mechanism:

  • When GMP Proves Accurate: For massive, heavily oversubscribed issues with transparent multi-broker anchor books (such as Waaree Energies or Swiggy), high GMP often correlates directionally with positive debut gains due to immense institutional demand.
  • When GMP Fails Disastrously: Because the grey market has extremely low liquidity and zero public audit trails, prices can easily be manipulated by small cartels or interested promoters seeking to artificially generate retail hype before bidding closes. Once bidding concludes, the artificial GMP frequently evaporates, resulting in severe listing day discounts.

Key Risks of Relying Solely on GMP

  1. Extreme Volatility: A sudden macro shift, geopolitical shock, or interest rate decision by the RBI or US Federal Reserve between bidding closure and listing day can cause GMP to crash within hours.
  2. Circular Artificial Quotations: Small dealer groups can quote high bids among themselves without actual volume execution, misleading retail investors into subscribing for weak fundamentally flawed issues.
  3. Zero Legal Recourse: Informal agreements carry 100% counterparty default risk.
