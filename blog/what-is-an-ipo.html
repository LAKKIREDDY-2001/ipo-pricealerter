What is an IPO? A Complete Beginner's Guide for Indian Investors

By IPO PriceAlerter Research • 8 min read • Updated: November 2024

Imagine a private Indian enterprise—perhaps a tech delivery company, a solar energy firm, or a specialty manufacturing business—that has spent years building operations, generating revenue, and serving customers. To scale into new geographic territories, repay legacy bank debts, invest in cutting-edge research and development, or allow early venture capital investors to exit, the company requires hundreds or even thousands of crores of fresh capital.

Instead of taking loans from commercial banks at high interest rates, the company chooses to transition from a privately held entity into a publicly listed enterprise by selling equity shares to institutional and retail investors. This monumental transition is known as an Initial Public Offering (IPO).

What Exactly is an Initial Public Offering (IPO)?

An Initial Public Offering (IPO) is the formal process by which a privately held company offers equity shares to the public for the first time, leading to its listing on a recognized stock exchange such as the National Stock Exchange (NSE) or the Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE).

Prior to the IPO, the company's shares are held exclusively by founders, early employees, angel investors, and private equity funds. Following a successful IPO and subsequent exchange debut, any retail citizen holding a Demat account and a bank account can buy, sell, and trade those shares on secondary market order books.

Why Do Companies Launch IPOs in India?

Corporations undergo the rigorous, multi-month regulatory and audit scrutiny required for a public listing for several strategic motivations:

  • Raising Growth Capital: A fresh equity issue allows companies to finance factory expansions, acquire competitors, or invest in new product lines without adding debt to their balance sheet.
  • Debt Reduction: High debt-to-equity ratios constrain cash flows due to interest obligations. Raising primary equity allows firms to pay down expensive debt.
  • Exit Route for Early Investors: Angel investors, venture capitalists, and promoters frequently monetize portions of their holdings through an Offer for Sale (OFS) component in the IPO.
  • Enhanced Credibility & Brand Awareness: Being a publicly traded company on NSE/BSE brings immense visibility, customer trust, and prestige among institutional partners.
  • Employee Retention: Listed companies can award liquid Employee Stock Option Plans (ESOPs), aiding in top-tier executive talent acquisition.
Key Distinction: Fresh Issue vs. Offer for Sale (OFS)

In a Fresh Issue, brand new shares are minted, and the proceeds flow directly into the company's bank accounts for operational use. In an Offer for Sale (OFS), existing shareholders (founders or private equity funds) sell their personal shares, and the cash proceeds go directly to those selling shareholders rather than the company.

How Does the IPO Process Work in India?

The journey from a private boardroom discussion to ringing the opening bell on the exchange floor is governed by strict regulatory oversight from the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI):

  1. Appointment of Merchant Bankers: The issuing company appoints Book Running Lead Managers (BRLMs) such as Kotak Mahindra Capital, Morgan Stanley, or ICICI Securities to manage valuations, underwriting, and legal compliance.
  2. Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP) Filing: The company files a detailed DRHP with SEBI, disclosing full 3-year audited financials, promoter shareholding, risk factors, outstanding litigations, and stated objectives.
  3. SEBI Review & Regulatory Approval: SEBI reviews the disclosures, issues observations, and demands necessary clarifications to protect retail investors.
  4. Red Herring Prospectus (RHP) & Price Band: Once approved, the company files the final RHP, establishing the Price Band (e.g., ₹371 to ₹390 per share) and minimum Lot Size (e.g., 38 shares).
  5. Bidding Window (T-Day to T+2): The IPO opens for bidding across three working days. Bidders submit electronic bids via UPI ASBA through their brokers.
  6. Allotment Finalization & Listing (T+3 Framework): Under SEBI's mandated T+3 settlement timeline, allotments are finalized within two business days, unallocated bank funds are unblocked, and shares debut on NSE and BSE on the third business day.

Types of IPOs: Mainboard vs. SME Platform

In India, public offerings operate across two primary segments:

  • Mainboard IPOs: Large, established corporations listing on the primary NSE/BSE boards. Minimum issue sizes typically exceed ₹100 Crores, retail lot sizes range around ₹14,000 to ₹15,000, and stringent quarterly audit disclosures apply.
  • SME Emerge IPOs: Small and Medium Enterprises listing on BSE SME or NSE Emerge. Designed for emerging businesses with post-issue capital up to ₹25 Crores. Retail minimum lot sizes are significantly larger (typically ₹1,00,000 to ₹2,00,000 per application) with lower liquidity.

Who Can Apply? Understanding Investor Categories

Every public issue reserves specific percentage quotas to ensure equitable allocation:

  • Retail Individual Investors (RII): Individuals applying for amounts up to ₹2,00,000. Under SEBI rules, when oversubscribed, allotment is determined through an automated randomized computerized lottery.
  • Non-Institutional Investors (NII / HNI): High Net-worth individuals applying for bids above ₹2 Lakh. Subdivided into Small NII (sNII) (₹2L to ₹10L) and Big NII (bNII) (>₹10L) with proportionate or lottery allotment rules.
  • Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIB): Mutual funds, insurance companies, banks, foreign institutional investors (FIIs), and pension funds. Typically receives 50% of the net issue.

Key Primary Market Terms Every Beginner Must Master

  • Price Band: The minimum (floor price) and maximum (cap price) between which investors can submit bids. Retail applicants should almost always select the Cut-Off Price (highest cap) to avoid invalidating their bid if the issue prices at the top.
  • Lot Size: The minimum number of shares an investor must bid for in a single application (e.g., 1 lot = 50 shares). You cannot buy individual fractional shares during the IPO bidding stage.
  • ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount): The secure banking mechanism where application funds remain in your bank account, earning regular savings interest, and are only debited if shares are successfully allotted to you.
  • Grey Market Premium (GMP): An unofficial, off-market sentiment indicator reflecting what dealers and traders are willing to pay above the issue price before official listing day. While helpful for gauging market demand, GMP carries zero regulatory guarantee.
