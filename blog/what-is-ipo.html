What is an IPO? A Complete Beginner's Guide for Indian Investors

By Venkata Madhava Reddy (Lead Financial Analyst) • 12 min read • Published: October 2026 • Updated: November 3, 2026

Imagine a private Indian enterprise—perhaps a pioneering renewable energy manufacturer, a national quick-commerce logistics network, or a specialty engineering firm—that has spent years bootstrapping operations, scaling revenues, and serving millions of customers. To expand factories into new industrial corridors, repay costly corporate bank debt, invest in cutting-edge research, or provide liquidity to early angel investors, the company needs hundreds or even thousands of crores in fresh equity capital.

Instead of relying on commercial bank loans that carry burdensome interest charges regardless of economic conditions, the enterprise chooses to transition from a private entity to a publicly traded corporation on recognized stock exchanges like the National Stock Exchange (NSE) or Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE). This milestone journey is called an Initial Public Offering (IPO).

Key Takeaway: The Primary Market vs Secondary Market

An IPO belongs to the Primary Market, where shares are created and purchased directly from the issuer. Once those shares list on the exchange floor, they transition to the Secondary Market, where investors trade existing shares among themselves through brokers like Zerodha, Groww, and Angel One without company involvement.

Why Do Companies Launch IPOs in India?

Launching a public offering requires months of intensive due diligence, audited financial disclosures, and strict regulatory vetting by the Securities and Exchange Board of India (SEBI). Indian corporations undertake this journey for five primary reasons:

  • Raising Equity Capital for Expansion: Fresh capital raised from institutional and retail investors can be deployed into capital expenditures (CapEx), acquiring competitors, expanding manufacturing capacity, or entering overseas markets without compounding balance-sheet debt.
  • Debt Deleveraging: High interest obligations restrict free cash flow. Allocating IPO proceeds toward retiring high-interest bank borrowings instantly strengthens profitability and improves credit ratings.
  • Liquidity for Founders & Venture Capital: Early angel investors, venture capitalists, and private equity funds who backed the startup need an exit mechanism. An Offer for Sale (OFS) enables them to monetize portions of their holdings transparently.
  • Brand Prestige & Institutional Credibility: A public listing brings immense visibility, quarterly governance transparency, and customer credibility that unlisted private companies cannot match.
  • Attracting Talent with ESOPs: Publicly traded shares give liquid value to Employee Stock Option Plans (ESOPs), enabling companies to attract and retain senior management talent.

Understanding the Core Components: Fresh Issue vs. Offer for Sale (OFS)

When you read an IPO prospectus (Red Herring Prospectus or RHP), the total issue size is almost always split into two components:

Feature Fresh Issue Offer for Sale (OFS)
Where does money go? Directly into the Company's Bank Account To Selling Shareholders (Promoters / PE)
Share Capital Effect Increases (New shares are minted) Remains Same (Ownership transfers)
EPS Impact Dilutes earnings per share initially No dilution in total share count

Investor Insight: As a general rule of thumb, retail investors favor issues with a substantial Fresh Issue proportion, because capital remains within the business to fund future revenue growth rather than simply cashing out promoters.

The Modern SEBI T+3 IPO Timeline in India

In late 2023, SEBI enforced the revolutionary T+3 listing timeline across all Indian public offerings, slashing the turnaround cycle from six days to just three business days after bidding closes:

  1. Bidding Window (T-Day to T+2): Public issues remain open for retail bidding for 3 full working days from 10:00 AM to 5:00 PM IST.
  2. Basis of Allotment (T+1): The registrar (Link Intime, KFintech, etc.) cleanses bids, conducts the randomized retail lottery, and publishes allotment status within 24 hours of issue closure.
  3. Demat Credit & Unblocking (T+2): Allotted shares are credited directly to winners' CDSL or NSDL Demat accounts. Unallocated funds are unblocked in bank accounts via UPI ASBA.
  4. Exchange Debut (T+3): Shares begin trading on the National Stock Exchange (NSE) and Bombay Stock Exchange (BSE) at 10:00 AM IST following the 9:00 AM to 9:45 AM pre-open discovery session.

Who Can Apply? The Three Investor Categories

Every Indian Mainboard IPO reserves distinct quotas to balance retail participation with institutional diligence:

  • Retail Individual Investors (RII): Resident individuals applying for amounts up to ₹2,00,000. Under SEBI rules, 35% of the net issue is reserved for retail applicants, and oversubscribed issues are distributed via computerized lottery where each winning PAN receives one minimum lot.
  • Non-Institutional Investors (NII / HNI): High Net-worth individuals applying for bids above ₹2 Lakh. Reserved 15% quota, subdivided into Small NII (₹2L to ₹10L) and Big NII (>₹10L).
  • Qualified Institutional Buyers (QIB): Mutual funds, domestic banks, foreign portfolio investors (FPIs), and insurance companies. Typically allocated 50% of the issue, including an Anchor Investor allocation finalized one day prior to opening.

Core Financial Ratios to Analyze Before Applying

Never bid on an IPO based solely on market rumors or social media hype. Always inspect these core financial health metrics in the Draft Red Herring Prospectus (DRHP):

  • Price-to-Earnings (P/E) Ratio: Compare the company's valuation multiple at the upper price band with established listed peers in the same industry.
  • Return on Net Worth (RoNW) & ROCE: Measures how efficiently promoter management generates profit from invested shareholder equity. Look for consistent figures above 15–20%.
  • Revenue & EBITDA Growth (3-Year CAGR): Ensure revenue growth is driven by core volume expansion rather than one-time accounting gains.
  • Debt-to-Equity Ratio: Companies with debt-to-equity below 1.0x possess superior shock absorption capacity during macroeconomic slowdowns.

Frequently Asked Questions (FAQ)

What is the difference between a Fresh Issue and an Offer for Sale (OFS)?

In a Fresh Issue, new shares are minted and proceeds flow directly into the company's accounts to fund expansion or pay down debt. In an Offer for Sale (OFS), existing promoters or early venture investors sell their personal shares and pocket the proceeds directly, with zero funds entering the company.

What happens to my money if I am not allotted shares in an IPO?

Under the ASBA (Application Supported by Blocked Amount) framework, your funds are never debited during bidding; they are merely blocked in your bank account, continuing to earn regular savings interest. If you receive no allotment, your bank automatically unblocks the lien on T+2 day.

What is the Cut-Off Price in an IPO application?

The Cut-Off Price is the highest price cap in the company's price band. Retail investors should always select the Cut-Off price checkbox to ensure their application is valid regardless of where the issue finally prices.
